\section{Population objective and possible estimation bias}
\label{app:ws_bias_analysis}

\subsection{Population-objective identity}
\label{app:ws_bias_population}

Using the notation of Appendix~\ref{app:fsm_innovation_energy}, let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bias_population_objective}
 \mathcal J(\theta)
 =\frac12\mathbb E[r_{\nu,\mathrm{FSM}}^2(t;\theta)]
\end{equation}
denote the population counterpart of the innovation-energy criterion.
The following identity is stated for stationary filtering and an exact
representation of the true signal path, under the assumptions of that
appendix. Define
\begin{align}
\label{eq:bias_population_definitions}
 \Delta\beta(t;\theta)
 &=\beta_{\mathrm{FSM}}(t;\theta^\star)
       -\beta_{\mathrm{FSM}}(t;\theta),\\
 d&=[d_1,\ldots,d_{n_d}]^\top,\qquad
 E^\star(t)=[e^\star(t-1),\ldots,e^\star(t-n_d)]^\top,\nonumber\\
 \Gamma_e&=\mathbb E[E^\star(t)E^\star(t)^\top].
\end{align}
The residual decomposition established there gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bias_population_identity}
 2\mathcal J(\theta)
 =\sigma_\nu^2
  +(d-d^\star)^\top\Gamma_e(d-d^\star)
  +\mathbb E\!\left[
    \left(D(z^{-1};\theta)\Delta\beta(t;\theta)\right)^2\right].
\end{equation}
The cross terms vanish because the input is independent of the
innovation process and $\nu(t)$ is orthogonal to past disturbances.
Consequently, under identifiability and $\Gamma_e\succ0$, the true
parameter $\theta^\star$ uniquely minimizes the population objective.
This statement concerns fixed candidate parameters; it does not imply
that estimates obtained from a finite noisy record are unbiased.
For a fixed FSM approximation that does not represent the true signal
path exactly, an additional approximation error must be considered.

\subsection{Reconstructed regressors and statistical bias}
\label{app:ws_bias_reconstruction}

In the PL formulation, the unmeasured internal signals and disturbances
are replaced by their reconstructions to build $\hat\Phi^{(k)}$.
These reconstructions and the innovation estimates are updated using
the same noisy output record. Their estimation errors can therefore
share noise with the regression residual, so the usual
regressor--error orthogonality need not hold. Regressor contamination
and the resulting bias of ordinary least squares are standard
errors-in-variables issues in system identification
\cite{Soderstrom2007}.

To see the mechanism, let $\varphi^\star(t)^\top$ be a row of the
ideal PL regressor and $\hat\varphi(t)^\top$ its reconstructed
counterpart at a given outer iteration. Since
$c(t)=\varphi^\star(t)^\top\theta^\star+\nu(t)$, the regression using
the reconstructed signals is
\begin{align}
\label{eq:bias_effective_regression_error}
 c(t)&=\hat\varphi(t)^\top\theta^\star+\varepsilon(t),\\
 \varepsilon(t)
 &=\nu(t)-[\hat\varphi(t)-\varphi^\star(t)]^\top\theta^\star.
\end{align}
The reconstruction error enters both $\hat\varphi(t)$ and
$\varepsilon(t)$. Hence, even though $\nu(t)$ is white, the effective
regression error need not be orthogonal to the reconstructed regressor.
If this correlation is present, least-squares updates can yield
statistically biased estimates.

Related block-oriented identification methods address this issue
using instruments constructed from noise-free signal counterparts,
together with iterative reconstruction of unmeasured internal signals
\cite{NiGilsonGarnier2013}. Janczak and Korbicz
\cite{JanczakKorbicz2019} give an explicit example in which
regressor--disturbance correlation makes a transformed Wiener
least-squares estimator inconsistent. Their disturbance placement
differs from the output-noise model considered here, so that result
illustrates the mechanism rather than proving bias of the present
algorithms.

Joint estimation alone does not establish bias. In particular,
dependence on past colored disturbances does not imply correlation
with the current white innovation. The relevant question is whether
the implemented reconstruction preserves orthogonality with its
effective regression error. This must be assessed for the estimator
actually used; the population identity alone does not settle its
finite-record bias.
